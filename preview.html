FAQ

Where the figures come from, and why some charts look the way they do.

Where does the data come from?

Directly from the SEC. Every figure is read from the filings companies submit to the SEC's EDGAR system: annual reports (10-K, 20-F, 40-F), quarterly reports (10-Q), and earnings releases (Form 8-K, Item 2.02). We read the tagged (XBRL) data in those filings and the statements they render.

We don't use third-party data providers, and we never estimate or fill in a number that wasn't filed. If a figure isn't in the filings, it isn't on the site.

Is a 10-K mandatory?

Yes. Every US company that reports to the SEC must file a 10-K once a year. It covers the full fiscal year and its financial statements are audited.

Is a 10-Q mandatory?

Today, yes. Since 1970, US public companies have filed a 10-Q for each of the first 3 quarters of their fiscal year; the 4th quarter is covered by the 10-K.

That may change. On May 5, 2026, the SEC proposed letting companies file one half-year report on a new Form 10-S instead of three 10-Qs, chosen each year by a checkbox on the 10-K. Companies that don't opt in would keep filing 10-Qs. At the time of writing (November 2026) it is still a proposal, not a final rule.

Source: SEC press release 2026-42

How are foreign companies different?

Foreign companies listed in the US (foreign private issuers) don't file 10-Ks or 10-Qs. They file an annual report on Form 20-F (Form 40-F for many Canadian companies), and their interim results, if any, are furnished on Form 6-K.

SEC rules already allow them to report every half-year instead of every quarter, and many only file annual results in tagged form. That's why some of them have half-year charts or no interim charts at all.

What do the small line charts show?
  • On a quarterly report: the last 5 quarters.
  • On an annual report: the last 5 quarters leading up to the year end, so you can see the trend inside the year.
  • For half-year filers: the last 3 half-years, marked "Half-year filer".

A line is only drawn when the quarters or half-years add up to the annual figure in the filing.

Why do some cards say "No chart because…"?

When we can't draw a line from the filed figures, the card says why instead of staying blank. Common reasons: the company files annual reports only, the metric is reported once a year, it isn't in the half-year figures, or the quarterly figures in the filings don't add up to the year.

How are the Upcoming dates worked out?

They're our forecast, not the company's announcement. We take the date of last year's earnings release for the same quarter (its Form 8-K, Item 2.02) and add 52 weeks, keeping the same day of the week and time of day. Companies sometimes move their dates, so check the company's own announcement before relying on one.

Where does the IPO list come from?

The SEC has no IPO calendar, so we build one from EDGAR. Each step of an IPO leaves a filing: the registration (S-1, or F-1 for foreign companies), an amendment with the price range, the SEC's notice that the registration is effective (EFFECT), and the final prospectus with the offering price (424B4).

No filing gives the pricing date in advance. The "Expected" date is our estimate: the day the latest price range was filed plus the median number of days recent IPOs took from that filing to pricing.

How often is the site updated?

On business days we check the SEC for new filings before the market opens, through the day and after the close, and new reports appear on the Latest page. Every night the whole library is rebuilt from the SEC's data.

Why isn't a company in the library?

Some filings can't be read cleanly enough to check that the figures add up. We'd rather leave a company out than show numbers that might be wrong. Use the feedback form on any company page to ask for one.

Where is my watchlist saved?

In a cookie in your browser only. Nothing is sent to us, and it won't follow you to another browser or device.

Is this investment advice?

No. The site shows what companies filed with the SEC, drawn as charts. It isn't a recommendation to buy or sell anything.
